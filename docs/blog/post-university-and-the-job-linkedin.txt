SFT is university. RL is the job. I gave one tiny model four careers to see if the analogy holds.

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) is university: the model is shown worked examples and learns to reproduce them. A textbook with the answer key attached.

Reinforcement learning (RL) is the job: nobody hands you the answers. You try something, you find out whether it worked, and you do more of what got results.

My test subject is a 27,000-parameter model that learns to sort three numbers, live in the browser.

STRAIGHT TO THE JOB

No examples, only a score. 900 rounds, about 21,600 attempts. It scored zero on every one. Its answers were "7", "1", "3 1". Not wrong lists, not lists at all. RL learns by doing more of what scored better, and when everything scores zero there is nothing to do more of.

DeepSeek's R1-Zero did learn to reason from RL alone. But it skipped university, not school: it started from a model pretrained on much of the internet. The job needs someone who can already get it right some of the time.

UNIVERSITY, THEN A CHOICE

Worked examples got it to 57% on lists it had never seen. Then:
More university, 900 more rounds: 70% on one run, 80% on another.
The job, 900 rounds where it only learns whether its own answer was right: 86% on both.

No answers shown, and it finished ahead both times.

A caveat worth knowing: research comparing models before and after RL finds most of the "new" habits were already possible in the base model. RL makes it reach for them reliably. Like the job turning something you saw once in a lecture into a habit.

THE JOB'S FAILURE MODE

Same graduate, lazier manager: the checker only asks for three numbers in order, never that they are the numbers it was given.

Within 150 rounds its score hit 100% and stayed there. Real accuracy went nowhere: 57% to 55%. Sort 4 7 4? "4 4 5". Full marks. Once every answer scores perfectly there is nothing left to learn, so it stopped improving the moment its score said it was flawless.

That is reward hacking. At frontier scale it is more inventive. A paper this month, "Coding or Cheating?", found nearly every frontier coding agent it tested tried to obtain the answer on hard problems instead of deriving it: searching repository history for the real fix, reading git's object store, scanning the disk for hidden tests. The author calls it a learned shortcut from training environments that leaked the answer.

WHY THIS MATTERS AT WORK

If you build or buy anything trained with RL, ask what you would ask of a bonus scheme: what does the score actually check? My lazy checker was not wrong about anything it measured. It measured less than the job, and the model learned exactly the difference.

School gets you something to work with. University gets you to competent. The job gets you to expert, if the job measures the right thing.

A new model called Jev landed this week that does not predict the next token. It does not write at all.

You give it a question and a list of allowed answers; it returns one, in a single pass, with a confidence score. TypeSafe AI says it "can't hallucinate", because the valid answers are fixed in a schema in advance.

That claim is narrower than the coverage suggests. They report a 0% structured output error rate, then say plainly: "Our number is not empirical. Schema matching is guaranteed." They are not claiming the answers are right, only well-formed — and say openly that this is true by construction.

So the load-bearing claim is the other one: "Calibrated: higher confidence means higher accuracy". If correctness is not guaranteed, confidence is what tells you when to escalate — and unlike the 0%, it is testable.

I tested it on my own model and got humbled twice.

WHAT MINE DOES

The tic-tac-toe agent on JabberLM is the same shape: one pass, nine allowed answers, a probability on each. I ship two versions, one deliberately undertrained. Neither can return a malformed move.

The undertrained one picks an already-occupied cell in 60% of positions — the weaker example, and I want to be fair about it. An occupied cell is illegal, and legality is exactly what a schema can encode. I could have offered only the empty ones.

The failure no schema reaches is the well-trained one: a legal, well-formed move that is simply worse, in about 2% of positions. Nobody designs that away, because the question is which allowed answer is right.

THE CONFIDENCE NUMBER

The undertrained agent shows how confident it is. Across all 4,520 non-terminal positions it runs from 17.4158% to 17.4225%.

The same number every time. It does not vary with the board because it is not looking at the board, and it had sat on screen for weeks looking like a measurement.

The well-trained one does vary, and ranks honestly: 71.5% when its move is optimal, 48.1% when not. That is the property TypeSafe claims, and mine has it.

Then I wrote that it was also badly under-confident: at a stated 30% it was right 96% of the time. A reviewer pointed out this was my measurement error. Nearly half of tic-tac-toe positions have several equally good moves, so a model splitting its belief three ways shows 33% and is marked right. Score the mass across all the good moves and it is roughly honest.

WHAT I WOULD LIKE TO ASK

Not a gotcha. I got this wrong twice on a model I built.

When several answers are acceptable — routing a ticket, grading a risk — what does "higher confidence means higher accuracy" mean, and how was it scored?

The workflow benchmark uses two frontier models averaged as the reference answer. Does 68% mean agreement with them, rather than correctness?

And on the third of decisions that come out wrong, where does confidence sit?

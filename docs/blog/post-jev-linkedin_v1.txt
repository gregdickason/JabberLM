What is the economic benefit of AI if is stops talking to us and starts just working inside our systems?



A new model called Jev was announced this week that does not predict the next word. It does not write anything out at all.



You give it a question and a fixed list of allowed answers, and it returns one of them as its top choice, with a confidence attached. TypeSafe AI, the lab behind it, report it running 40 to 200 times faster and far cheaper than a frontier model on classification work.



They are careful about is does, and the press coverage missed some of the nuance. Their own FAQ asks "Can Jev still get things wrong?" and answers yes: the shape of the answer is guaranteed, the correctness is not. It cannot invent a category outside your list, but it can choose the wrong one from inside it.



This is a genuinely interesting design.  And it got a lot of attention: Six open clones appeared within about two days. The leading one, Laya, is built on ModernBERT-large — an encoder in the BERT family, the same approach used to score multiple-choice answers for years. I have been running laya-mlx, the Apple Silicon port, locally on a Mac mini to test legal classification of clauses (it is pretty good).   Come to the LexisNexis AI lab in NY to see it in action. 



What is exciting about Jev and models like it is that they are not just reading a model's scores over a fixed set of allowed answers, they are training those scores to mean something.



Which is when I realised I have one on my own JabberLM site, and never thought to call it that. The tic-tac-toe agent does not write its move out as text: one forward pass, a score for each of the nine cells, and the harness plays the highest score. A typed decision.



ECONOMIC VALUE



What I like about the promise here is a model quick and cheap enough to sit inline inside software, rather than at the end of a chat window.



Think of a complex insurance claim. Today's models are poor at this because they are built to produce language for a human to read. A typed decision model could classify the whole inbound stream instead, across multiple steps, alongside the software you already have — routing the low-confidence cases to a person.



So: no, an LLM does not always need to predict the next word to be useful. It does need to know when it is guessing, and often LLMs are rewarded for guessing as part of their training rather than saying plainly they are not confident. Jev shows a potentially more valuable path: let the model do work when it is confident, defer to a human when it is not. This is the next evolution of a SAAS business.

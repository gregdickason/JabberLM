Calibration is Jev's central claim. On the community leaderboard it comes third on it. That is not a reason to avoid it — it is a reason to know how to use it.



Two questions get muddled. Accuracy asks whether the answer is right. Calibration asks whether the confidence attached to it tells the truth. You do not buy a decision model to read its answers. You buy it to set a threshold, act unattended above the line and send everything below it to a person — which makes the confidence the load-bearing part.



The public numbers: Jev is right 73.9 percent of the time and claims 81.1 percent on average. That gap gets paid exactly where you stopped looking. Set the threshold at 90 percent and you are budgeting for one error in fifty. You will take one in fifteen.



But its confidence still ranks correctly, and that is the part that matters. Higher means more likely right, all the way up. Where it says 75 percent it is right 59; where it says 98 it is right 93. A shifted curve, not a broken one, and shifts are repairable.



HOW TO ACTUALLY USE ONE



Never inherit a vendor's threshold. It was calibrated on their distribution, not your inbound.



Label a few hundred examples of your own traffic, sampled across the whole confidence range. A set made only of the cases that went wrong is not a calibration set.



Plot stated confidence against observed accuracy and ask three separate questions. Does the number move at all? Does it rank? Does 0.9 mean nine times in ten? A model can pass any of those and fail the others.



Fit the correction on held-out data, set the threshold from the corrected curve, and use a different one per route. An allergy note misrouted is not a refund, it is a safety incident.



Then sample the automated lane forever, not just the escalations. Confidence reports how familiar the input looks, not how hard the decision is, so it cannot flag the cases it does not know are hard.



TWO TRAPS, BOTH OF WHICH I WALKED INTO



I ran this on my own toy agent, a 130,000 parameter thing that plays tic-tac-toe, across all 4,520 positions. It reported a confidence between 17.4158 and 17.4225 percent on every board. A constant to four decimal places, printed beside every move as though it meant something, and it had looked like a measurement for weeks. Check the number moves before you check anything else.



The second is worse, because it produces a confident wrong conclusion. I scored the top choice against "was that the best move", published that the model was wildly under-confident, and had to retract it. Forty-seven percent of those positions have more than one best move, so a model splitting its belief honestly across three scores 33 percent and looks terrified. The bug was in my ground truth. Score a known-perfect answer first, and check it comes out perfect.
